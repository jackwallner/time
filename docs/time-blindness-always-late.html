Time Blindness: Why You're Always Late and What Helps

By Jack Wallner · Last updated October 18, 2026

What is time blindness?

Time blindness is an everyday name for consistent difficulty sensing how much time has passed, how long something will take, and in what order things need to happen. CHADD, the national ADHD nonprofit, describes it in Attention magazine as a trait rather than a formal diagnosis, and notes that some researchers suggest it may be a characteristic of ADHD. You can run late without it, and you can have it without a diagnosis.

Why are you always late?

Because people guess too low, and for people with ADHD, timing itself can differ too.

Everyone guesses too low

In a classic 1994 study, Buehler, Griffin and Ross asked 37 psychology students when they would finish their honors thesis. They predicted 33.9 days on average and took 55.5. Fewer than a third (29.7%) finished within the time they had predicted. The researchers called this the planning fallacy: people picture the plan going smoothly and skip over how long similar tasks took them before. In a later experiment, the optimistic bias disappeared when people were told to connect their predictions to relevant past experience.

Timing really is different in ADHD

A 2022 meta-analysis of 55 studies in the Journal of the American Academy of Child & Adolescent Psychiatry found broad timing differences in people with ADHD, including less accurate time estimation and time production. Most of those tasks measure durations of seconds, not the 20 minutes it takes to get out the door, so the study does not prove why any one person is late. It does show the experience is real and measurable.

A guess is not a measurement

"Getting ready takes 45 minutes" is a guess. If you have never timed it, the guess is the only thing your plan rests on, and it is the one input the research says is optimistic.

What helps with time blindness?

Replace guesses with measured times, add buffer, make time visible and work backward from a fixed leave time. CHADD's list of strategies is a good starting point. Several are directly about lateness:

  • Make time visible. Visual timers, calendars and alarms.
  • Record how long typical tasks take you instead of relying on a guess.
  • Double or triple your time estimate, and add buffer time to tasks and breaks.
  • Break big tasks into smaller steps.
  • Schedule alerts for tasks and meetings.
  • Prepare the night before and keep a checklist by the door.

How do you run a one-week lateness experiment?

Time one routine for a week, then plan with your slower times instead of your guesses.

  1. Pick one routine you are often late for, such as getting out the door in the morning.
  2. Write each step and your guess for it.
  3. For a week, note how long each step really took.
  4. Plan with your slower times rather than your average. An average is late about half the time.
  5. Work backward from the time you must walk out, and set your alert for when to start, not when to leave.
  6. Add a buffer, then adjust after another week.

Worked example: you must leave at 8:15 and guess 45 minutes of getting ready. Your measured time is 68 minutes. Your real start time is 7:07, not 7:30.

Frequently asked questions

Is time blindness a real condition?

Not as a formal diagnosis: time blindness is a descriptive term. CHADD describes it as a trait and notes that some researchers suggest it may be a characteristic of ADHD. A 2022 meta-analysis of 55 studies found broad timing differences in people with ADHD.

Why am I always late even when I try?

Part of it is the planning fallacy: people predict how long tasks will take by imagining the best case. In a 1994 study, students predicted 33.9 days for a thesis and took 55.5, and under 30% finished in their predicted time.

What is the fastest way to stop being late?

Replace your guess with a measured time, work backward from the time you must leave, and add a buffer. CHADD suggests recording how long typical tasks take you, adding buffer time, and scheduling alerts.

Does time blindness mean I have ADHD?

No. Running late has many causes, and time blindness alone is not a diagnosis. If it is causing problems at work, school or home, a health care professional can evaluate why.

Can an app fix time blindness?

No. An app cannot fix it, but it can make the plan more realistic. Shoes On learns how long each step really takes you and tells you when to start. It is a planning aid and does not treat any condition.

Is it true that people with ADHD are always late?

No. A 2022 meta-analysis of 55 studies found broad timing differences in people with ADHD, but most of those tasks measured seconds, so it does not explain why any one person is late. Running late has many causes, and the planning fallacy makes almost everyone underestimate how long things take.

Can you have time blindness without ADHD?

Yes. Time blindness is an everyday term rather than a diagnosis, and CHADD notes only that some researchers suggest it may be a characteristic of ADHD. You can have it without any diagnosis. If it is causing real problems, a health care professional can help you work out why.
