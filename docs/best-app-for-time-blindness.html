Tiimo: To-Do List Planner

Tiimo is a visual planner co-designed with ADHD and autism experts, per its listing, and was named iPhone App of the Year 2025. It has a color-coded daily schedule, a to-do list with AI priority grouping, an AI co-planner, a focus timer, widgets and sync with Apple Calendar and Reminders. The free version includes a basic planner, limited AI chats and one profile. The trade-off: it plans your whole day, so getting out the door on time is one task among many rather than its focus.

Routinery (Routine Planner, Habit Tracker)

Routinery breaks a routine into short steps, each on its own timer, while a voice reads out what comes next, so it keeps running while you shower or cook. Its listing says 2 routines are free with no account, and that the free plan shows ads. It works on iPhone, iPad, Watch and Mac. The trade-offs: you set each step's length yourself, and routine data is stored on Routinery's servers, per its listing.

Time Timer

The app version of the red disk timer used in classrooms and therapy for decades. Watching the disk shrink makes time visible, which is the whole idea. Free: up to 3 timers, run at once if you like. The premium upgrade adds timer sequencing for routines, groups and sync. The trade-off: it is a timer, so it does not know when you need to leave, and its last update was March 20, 2026.

Lately: Be On Time

Lately is built around the moment you must leave. It shows a visual timer on the Lock Screen, the Dynamic Island and Apple Watch, estimates travel time with traffic and your travel method, sends several alerts before the leave time and sounds an alarm at it. Points and badges reward arriving on time. The trade-off: its listing describes timers and alerts for the leave time rather than a step-by-step getting-ready routine, and it has a small number of ratings.

Shoes On (my app)

You set when you walk out the door and the steps before it, and Shoes On works backward to tell you when to start. You say how far your guesses usually run over, then it learns each step's real time, your overall pace and how long you take to get going after the alert. While you get ready, one screen, the Lock Screen and the Dynamic Island show your step and whether you are behind. It sends a get-ready alert, follow-ups if you miss it, and offers to drop a later step when you fall behind. Free: one routine with every alert, the widget and all the learning. Its limits: no travel-time or traffic estimate (you set the leave time), more routines and the Apple Watch coach need Shoes On Pro, there is no iPad app, and it launched this week with no ratings yet.

Which should you pick?

If your whole day slips, pick Tiimo. If you always underestimate how long getting ready takes, pick Shoes On.

  • Your whole day slips, not only the mornings: Tiimo.
  • You know the steps but stall between them, and a voice prompt helps: Routinery.
  • You need to see time pass during a task: Time Timer.
  • Your lateness comes from the trip itself, and traffic varies: Lately.
  • You always think getting ready takes less time than it does: Shoes On.

Frequently asked questions

What is time blindness?

Time blindness is an informal term for trouble sensing how much time has passed or how long a task will take. It comes up often in conversations about ADHD, but it is not a diagnosis on its own.

Which app helps you leave the house on time?

Lately and Shoes On are both built around a leave time. Lately estimates travel time and alerts you before you need to go. Shoes On plans the getting-ready steps backward from the door and learns how long each one really takes you.

Are there free apps for time blindness?

Yes. All five here are free to download, with paid upgrades. Routinery has 2 routines free with ads, Time Timer has up to 3 timers free, Tiimo has a basic planner free, and Shoes On has one full routine free.

Does Shoes On work on Apple Watch?

Yes, with Shoes On Pro. The Watch coach shows your current step and pace and lets you tap Done. The free version runs on iPhone with a Lock Screen view, a widget and alerts.

Is this comparison independent?

How do you get better with time blindness?

Replace guesses with measured times and make time visible. CHADD, the national ADHD nonprofit, suggests visual timers and alarms, recording how long typical tasks take you, adding buffer, breaking tasks into smaller steps and scheduling alerts. Apps can carry some of that load: Time Timer shows time passing, Routinery runs a routine step by step, and Lately and Shoes On plan around the time you must leave.
